# The Best Plugins for Adobe Photoshop in 2025

On 30 July 2026 I opened fifteen Photoshop plugin vendor pages and checked them one at a time. Five are discontinued, unbuyable or gone. Two sell a job Photoshop now does itself. One I could not find a legitimate download for anywhere.

I run Pixel Street, a web design and branding studio in Salt Lake, Kolkata. We design and build for brands like Coca-Cola, ITC and Marico, and Photoshop is open on someone’s machine here most working days. So this is not a research exercise. A plugin list that has rotted is worse than no list, because it sends a designer to a payment page for software nobody is maintaining.

Here is the argument I would make to anyone still shopping for plugins in 2026.

## The short version

- **Adobe eats plugin categories.** A plugin proves a feature is worth money, Adobe ships it in the base app, and the plugin becomes a rounding error. Sky replacement, upscaling, background removal and compositing have all gone that way, and the last of those went in October 2025.
- **Four of the fifteen survive unchanged.** Everything else is renamed, discontinued, converted to subscription, absorbed by Adobe, or was never a verifiable product.
- **The survivors have something in common.** They do a narrow, unglamorous job that Adobe has no commercial reason to build: grids, noise, brushes, colour panels. Nothing in that list is exciting, which is exactly why it is still there.
- **The install instructions you will find almost everywhere are wrong.** You cannot copy a modern plugin file into the Plug-ins folder and expect it to load. The routes that do work are below.
- **My honest recommendation is to install fewer plugins than you think.** Every panel you add is a dependency on a company that may not renew its certificate.

## What Photoshop now does that you used to buy a plugin for

This is the part of the story that makes a plugin roundup age badly, and it is worth being specific about, because three of the recommendations above were selling a capability Adobe now includes.

**Sky replacement.** Adobe announced Sky Replacement for Photoshop at MAX on 20 October 2020. [Digital Camera World’s coverage that day](https://www.digitalcameraworld.com/news/adobe-max-2020-all-the-new-features-in-photoshop-lightroom-and-premiere-pro) put it plainly: “Skylum Luminar was the first software to successfully (and almost magically) replace dull skies with new ones, and now Photoshop will use Sensei to pull off a similar trick.” Sky replacement was Luminar AI’s headline feature. Skylum stopped selling Luminar AI two years later. That is the whole pattern in one paragraph, written by somebody who was watching it happen.

**Upscaling.** At Adobe MAX on 28 October 2025, Adobe announced Generative Upscale in Photoshop, and it does not run on an Adobe model alone. Adobe’s own announcement says the feature “leverages Topaz Labs’ AI” to turn small or low-resolution images into 4K. Two of the plugins on this page, Topaz Gigapixel and Blow Up 3, exist to enlarge images. One of them is now partly inside Photoshop, licensed from the same company that sells the plugin.

**Compositing.** The same release added Harmonize, which Adobe describes as blending “people or objects into new scenes, matching light, color and tone”. That is a large part of what a retouching panel sold.

**Generative fill and remove.** These now run against a choice of models rather than only Adobe’s. Adobe named Google’s Gemini 2.5 Flash Image and Black Forest Labs’ FLUX.1 Kontext alongside its own Firefly models in the MAX 2025 announcement.

None of that makes plugins pointless. It makes a specific kind of plugin pointless: the kind whose pitch is “an effect Photoshop cannot do yet”. If a plugin’s value proposition is a gap in Adobe’s feature set, you are renting time until Adobe closes it. I have written the longer version of this argument, applied across the whole design toolchain, in [which AI design tools actually survived](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/the-ai-revolution-in-the-design-sector/).

There is a catch worth naming, because it changes the arithmetic rather than removing it. Adobe’s generative features run on credits, and the credits are metered. In its write-up of the October 2025 release, [CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2025/10/adobe-releases-photoshop-27-0/) reported that a Photoshop subscription carries 25 free generative credits a month, that an Adobe-model upscale costs 5 credits below 9 megapixels and 10 above it, and that routing the same job through the Topaz models costs 10 or 20. So the feature is in the box, but heavy use is not free, and a perpetual plugin licence is not automatically the more expensive option. Work out your own volume before you cancel anything.

## What this means if you design websites rather than photographs

Look again at those fifteen and notice how few of them were for us. Two: Ink, for generating layer specs to hand to a developer, and GuideGuide, for grids. Ink is the one that died.

That is not a coincidence and it is not really Ink’s fault. Design handoff stopped happening in Photoshop. Specs, tokens, redlines and component states moved to tools built for interfaces, and a plugin whose entire job was to compensate for Photoshop not being an interface tool had nowhere to go. If you are still generating web specs out of Photoshop layers in 2026, the plugin is not your problem.

Photoshop still earns its place in our work, but for a narrower set of jobs than a plugin roundup tends to imply: preparing and correcting photography, compositing hero imagery, cleaning up client-supplied assets that arrive as flattened JPEGs, and producing the export variants a build actually needs. For that work the plugins that matter are the boring ones. Grids, noise, colour.

The rest of the layout conversation has moved on entirely, and the tooling decisions that matter now are about which model you let near a client deliverable and which canvas your team lives in. That argument, and what held up when I checked it, is in [what actually survived the hype in AI web design](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/artificial-intelligence-in-web-design/).

## The plugins I would still install

Surviving is not the same as being worth installing. Three of the four entries that came through unchanged did so because nothing has happened to them in years, which is a different thing from being maintained. The test I actually apply is narrower: does this do something Adobe has no commercial reason to build, and is somebody still answering the phone?

Four pass. None of them is exciting.

### GuideGuide, for grids

Setting up a column grid by hand in Photoshop is the kind of task that is too small to complain about and big enough to cost an hour a week. GuideGuide does it in a keystroke and works across Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign, After Effects and Figma on one licence. It converted to an annual subscription, and at $9 a year on the cheapest tier I have no argument to make against that.

The tiers are identical in what they give you. You pick the price. That is an unusual and honest way to sell software, and I would rather support it than not.

### Noiseware, for noise

Noise reduction is a solved problem with a lot of bad solutions. Imagenomic has been shipping Noiseware for long enough that the presets are worth the licence on their own. At $99.95 it is the most expensive thing on my surviving list, and it is the one I would defend hardest for anyone handling low-light photography.

### ParticleShop, for brushes

Corel’s particle brushes are genuinely difficult to replicate, because they are a port of Painter’s brush engine rather than a filter. If you do illustration work inside Photoshop, this is the one plugin here that Adobe shows no sign of building. I could not read Corel’s current price on its own page, so I am not quoting one.

### Nik Collection, if you edit photographs seriously

Nik Collection 9 is a real photo-editing suite rather than a plugin, and DxO has kept developing it. One correction worth making, because the number moves around: it is seven tools, not eight. DxO’s pricing did not render to automated fetch, so check it yourself before you buy.

### The one I would not buy, even though you still can

Exposure Software still takes money for Exposure X7, Blow Up 3 and Snap Art. All three pages work, all three have a buy button. The company’s own blog has not published since 7 November 2022, the version number has not moved, and Blow Up’s requirements page still talks about Photoshop CS6.

A live payment page is not evidence of a living product. If you already own these, keep using them until they break. I would not start a new dependency there, and I would certainly not put one in a client’s production workflow.

Everything else among the fifteen is either dead, free, discontinued or a capability Photoshop has absorbed.

## Three that are not real products any more

These three still get named in plugin conversations. None of them can be bought from anyone who wrote them.

**Retouch Pro.** I looked for a company, a product page and a purchase route. There is none. There is a well-known retouching forum with a similar name, and there are download sites distributing a cracked panel called “Retouch Pro Panel”. Installing software whose only distribution channel is a warez site is the single worst thing you can do to a machine holding client files.

**Luminar AI and Topaz B&W Effects.** Both discontinued by their own makers. Topaz’s legacy page is blunt about what that means: these programs may stop working as operating systems change, and support is limited.

**Ink and Photolemur.** One serves a 404, the other redirects to a different company’s product. Neither can be bought.

Nothing on this page is ranked, incidentally. There is no test behind an order, and any list that presents one is inventing it. What you get here is what I checked and what I found.

## How you actually install a Photoshop plugin in 2026

The instructions in circulation tell you to extract a ZIP, find a file ending in `.8bf` or `.zxp`, and copy it into Photoshop’s Plug-ins folder. Half of that is right and the half that is wrong will waste your afternoon.

1. **A filter with a `.8bf` extension** is a compiled plugin of the old kind. It does go in the Plug-ins folder, and it does appear under the Filter menu after a restart. Noiseware and Texture Anarchy are this kind.
2. **A panel with a `.zxp` extension** does not. Copying one into Plug-ins achieves nothing. Adobe’s developer documentation is explicit that acquisition and installation now run through the marketplace: “the Creative Cloud desktop app Plugin Marketplace and Exchange Marketplace are two different surfaces of the Creative Cloud plugin marketplace”. One is the Marketplace tab in the Creative Cloud app, the other is Adobe Exchange on the web. Use either. Do not hand-place the file.
3. **A modern plugin** is usually installed by the vendor’s own installer, which places it correctly and registers it with Photoshop. Run the installer. Do not go looking for files.
4. **Restart Photoshop.** That part has always been true and still is.

There is a second thing worth knowing before you commit to a panel. Photoshop has two extension architectures: the older CEP, which is what “Window > Extensions (Legacy)” loads, and UXP, which is what Adobe is building on now. Adobe’s own developer documentation says of CEP that “while it’s still going to be around in Photoshop for some time, it will at some point be deprecated and then, eventually, removed from Photoshop”.

That is a stated intention to remove, not a rumour. If a panel has not been updated in several years and still describes itself as being “for Photoshop CC”, treat its remaining life as finite. Two of the survivors on this page fall into that bracket.

## Where to download plugins without getting burned

[![Where to Download Best Photoshop Plugins from](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2024/09/Top-15-Photoshop-Plugins-2024.png)](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2024/09/Top-15-Photoshop-Plugins-2024.png)

My rule is short: buy from the company that wrote the software, or from Adobe. Everything else is a risk you are taking on behalf of a client’s files.

- **[Adobe Exchange](https://exchange.adobe.com/).** The official listing, reachable from the Marketplace tab of the Creative Cloud desktop app. Installation is handled for you and updates arrive through Creative Cloud. Start here.
- **The vendor’s own site.** Every survivor on this page sells directly. Buying direct also means you find out immediately when a company has stopped answering, which is information the resellers will not give you.
- **[Envato Elements](https://elements.envato.com/).** A subscription library of assets, actions, brushes and layer styles rather than compiled plugins. Envato’s own pricing page starts its Core tier at $16.50 a month. Useful if you consume a lot of stock; not a plugin store.
- **[GraphicRiver](https://graphicriver.net/).** Still trading as part of Envato Market, and still the place for one-off purchases of individual add-ons.

What I will not send you to is a site whose business model is hosting other people’s installers. That is where something like Retouch Pro lives, and nothing there belongs on a machine holding client work.

## Common questions

### Are Photoshop plugins still worth it in 2026?

For narrow, repetitive craft tasks, yes. For anything an AI feature can do, increasingly no, because Adobe keeps shipping those features into the base app and licensing outside models to do it. Buy plugins for grids, brushes, noise and colour. Do not buy plugins for effects.

### Which of these plugins are dead?

Retouch Pro was never a verifiable product. Ink’s site returns a 404. Photolemur redirects to Skylum’s Luminar. Luminar AI was withdrawn from sale in October 2022. Topaz B&W Effects is on Topaz’s own legacy list. That is five of the fifteen.

### Does Photoshop now include an image upscaler?

Yes. Generative Upscale shipped in the October 2025 release, and Adobe’s announcement credits Topaz Labs’ models as one of the options behind it. It consumes generative credits, which is the trade against a plugin you own outright.

### Why did my old Photoshop panel stop appearing?

Most likely it is a CEP extension and lives under Window > Extensions (Legacy) rather than the Plug-ins menu. Adobe has stated that CEP will eventually be removed from Photoshop, so a panel that has not been rebuilt on UXP is on a clock, whatever its vendor says.

### Do I need plugins to do professional web design work?

No, and I would go further. Most of our layout work does not happen in Photoshop at all any more, and the plugin that used to bridge Photoshop to development handoff is the one on this list that died. If you are choosing where to spend attention, the process matters more than the panel, and I have written out how we actually run a build in [our web design process](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/web-design-process-guide/).

## What I would do with this list

Install GuideGuide. Install Noiseware if you shoot in bad light. Install ParticleShop if you illustrate. Download Texture Anarchy, because it is free now. Then stop, and spend the rest of the budget on the base Photoshop subscription you are already paying for, because that is where the capability is arriving.

The wider lesson is the one I keep relearning. I lost two companies before Pixel Street, and the thing that survived both failures is a suspicion of anything I depend on but do not control. A plugin is a small version of that problem. It is somebody else’s code, on somebody else’s release schedule, with somebody else’s decision about whether to renew the domain. Five of the fifteen here are the proof.

I checked every product on this page on 30 July 2026 and I will check them again, because on this evidence about a third will be gone or renamed by 2028. If you find one that has died since, that is my error to fix.
