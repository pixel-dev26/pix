# Best Sites for Learning Web Design, Checked in 2026

**Summary.** If you are starting from zero with no budget, you do not need to spend anything at all. Work through freeCodeCamp's Responsive Web Design certification, use The Odin Project for the order to learn things in, and keep MDN open as your reference. I run Pixel Street, a web design studio in Salt Lake, Kolkata. We design and build for Coca-Cola, ITC and Marico. Selling you on the idea that web design is too hard to learn yourself would be good for my business. I am going to argue the opposite, because the only thing that makes a list like this worth reading is whether the links still work.

A learning list with rotten links is worse for a beginner than no list at all, because a beginner cannot tell the difference between a dead resource and their own failure to understand it. Roundups do not get re-checked, and the links quietly go: a company folds, a channel changes subject, a domain gets parked.

So I opened every outbound link on this page on **30 July 2026**, checked what each platform is now, and rebuilt the list around what survived. Every price below was printed on that company's own pricing page on that date. Where a company would not show me a price without an account, I have said so rather than guess. Rupee figures are what those sites quote to a visitor in India; dollar figures are what they quote in dollars.

## The short answer

If you are starting from zero with no budget, you do not need to spend anything at all. Work through freeCodeCamp's Responsive Web Design certification, use The Odin Project for the order to learn things in, and keep MDN open as your reference. That combination is a complete front-end education and all three are free.

Paid platforms earn their money for one reason: a gap you have already identified and can name. "I cannot get my head around CSS layout" is a gap. "I want to learn web design" is not, and a subscription will not fix it. Pay after you have hit a wall, not before.

One thing to settle before you start: decide whether you want to design websites or build them. They are related jobs with different day-to-day work, and we have written up the difference in [web design versus web development](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/web-design-vs-web-development/). Most of the resources below teach both, badly labelled.

## What has died, moved or changed hands

This is the part no other roundup will tell you, so it goes first.

- **Lynda.com no longer exists as a brand.** Going to lynda.com now returns a 301 redirect to linkedin.com/learning. The library survived the move; the name did not. Watch out for `lynda.com.cach3.com`, which was never Lynda at all: it is a third-party static archive of the site as it looked in May 2019, and the page says so at the top.
- **Udemy is now owned by Coursera.** Coursera announced the all-stock combination on 17 December 2025 and closed it on 11 May 2026, at an implied equity value of roughly $2.5 billion. Udemy is a wholly owned subsidiary of Coursera, and the companies have said the two platforms will keep running separately while they work on a unified product.
- **WebDesign.com is a parked domain.** It is still recommended in places as a YouTube channel to follow. The domain now resolves to a holding page owned by Digimedia.com, L.P. carrying a one-line definition of the phrase "web design".
- **TheNewBoston has moved on.** Bucky Roberts' channel is still uploading, which is why a link checker would call it healthy. The three most recent uploads when I checked were episodes one, two and three of a bonsai gardening series from March 2026. It is not a programming channel any more.
- **1stWebDesigner's channel is effectively dormant.** One upload in June 2025, and before that nothing since September 2020.
- **Codecademy belongs to Skillsoft.** The acquisition completed on 4 April 2022 and the footer of the site now reads "Codecademy from Skillsoft". The product is still good; the pricing has moved a long way in the years since people started recommending it.
- **Flux Academy sells something completely different now.** The 10k Website Process and The 6-Figure Freelance Designer are no longer sold under those names, and the $130 course tier is gone.
- **Frontend Masters renamed itself Master.dev** on 15 June 2026, on the grounds that "frontend" no longer described a catalogue that includes Go, Rust, cloud and AI. Old frontendmasters.com links still redirect, for now.

## Free, and better than most of the paid courses

### freeCodeCamp

![Freecodecamp-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Freecodecamp.png)

_Source: [freecodecamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/)_

freeCodeCamp now runs eleven certifications, all free, and each is built around roughly 300 hours of work and five projects you have to make pass a test suite. Start with **Responsive Web Design**. It covers semantic HTML, forms, accessibility, Flexbox and CSS Grid, which is the actual foundation of the job rather than a tour of it.

The reason I put this first is not the price. It is that the certification projects force you to produce finished work instead of watching someone else produce it. If you want to understand why the responsive part matters so much, we have a longer piece on [responsive web design](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/responsive-web-design/) and what it involves in practice.

After Responsive Web Design, the natural next steps in their catalogue are JavaScript Algorithms and Data Structures, then Front End Development Libraries.

### The Odin Project

[The Odin Project](https://www.theodinproject.com/) is a free, open-source curriculum that solves the problem freeCodeCamp does not: what to learn next, and in what order. It curates the best tutorials, articles and documentation that already exist and strings them into two paths, Full Stack JavaScript and Full Stack Ruby on Rails, with portfolio projects between the sections.

If you have ever opened twenty browser tabs, read four contradictory opinions on how to start, and closed all of them, this is the fix. Take the Full Stack JavaScript path unless you have a specific reason to want Rails.

### MDN Web Docs

MDN's [Learn web development](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development) section is free, maintained by Mozilla and the MDN community, and is the closest thing the web has to an official manual. The core modules run from structuring content with HTML through CSS styling, CSS layout, dynamic scripting with JavaScript, accessibility, design for developers and version control.

Treat MDN as your reference rather than your course. When a tutorial tells you to write something and you want to know what that thing actually does, MDN is where you find out. It is the one link in this post I expect to still be correct in a decade.

### W3Schools

[W3Schools](https://www.w3schools.com/) has been running since 1999 and covers 60-odd languages with free tutorials and a try-it-yourself editor. It is unbeatable for answering one narrow syntax question in fifteen seconds, and it is a poor primary curriculum because it teaches you what is possible without teaching you what is wise. The site also now sells a W3Schools Plus subscription and paid certificates; I did not quote a price because I could not read one on their page.

### YouTube

![YouTube-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/YouTube.png)

_Source: [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/)_

YouTube recommendations rot faster than anything else on a list like this, as the dormant and repurposed channels above show. The one I would still point a beginner at is [Flux Academy](https://www.youtube.com/@FluxAcademy), which posted on the day I checked and posts most weeks. Ran Segall's videos are strongest on design judgement and the commercial side of the work, which is exactly the part written tutorials tend to skip.

The [W3Schools channel](https://www.youtube.com/@w3schools) still exists but has not published since February 2025, so use the website instead.

My rule for YouTube generally: check the upload date before you follow a tutorial. A CSS video from 2019 will teach you workarounds for problems that no longer exist.

## Paid platforms, and what each one actually buys you

### Codecademy

![Codeacademy - home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Codeacademy.png)

_Source: [codecademy](https://www.codecademy.com/)_

Codecademy's strength is still the in-browser editor: you type, it checks, you move on. That loop is genuinely good for a first week when a local development setup would only get in the way.

The free tier is thinner than its reputation. Codecademy's free plan is widely described as giving you access to most of the content. It does not. Basic gives you basic access to free courses, community support and reference material. Skill paths, career paths, real-world projects, assessments and certificates of completion all sit behind the paywall. Plus is Rs 300 a month billed annually, Pro is Rs 600 a month billed annually, and both roughly double if you pay monthly. Codecademy has been owned by Skillsoft since April 2022.

### Treehouse

![Treehouse-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Treehouse.png)

_Source: [treehouse](https://teamtreehouse.com/)_

Treehouse runs more than 60 tracks, and the ones relevant here are the 42-hour Web Design track, Front End Web Development, Design and Build a Website, and Web Programming Skills for Graphic Designers. That last one is the most interesting entry on their catalogue if you already design and want to stop handing files over a wall.

Pricing is Rs 1,650 a month for Courses and Rs 3,250 a month for Courses Plus, with a seven-day free trial. Their Techdegree bootcamp programmes are priced separately at $199 a month, which is a very different commitment and should be treated as one.

### Coursera, and Udemy

![Courseera-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Courseera.png)

_Source: [coursera](https://www.coursera.org/)_

Coursera works with more than 350 university and industry partners and puts over 10,000 courses behind Coursera Plus, which costs Rs 2,099 a month or Rs 13,999 a year. What you are buying is the name on the certificate. Google's and Meta's UX and front-end programmes live here, and that branding is worth something on a CV in a way that a platform-branded certificate usually is not.

![Udemy-Home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Udemy.png)

_Source:[udemy](https://www.udemy.com/)_

Udemy is now part of the same company. The merger closed on 11 May 2026 and Udemy operates as a wholly owned subsidiary of Coursera, with both platforms running separately for the moment. The model still differs: Udemy is a marketplace where you buy one course outright rather than subscribing, which suits a single narrow topic you want to own permanently.

I am not quoting Udemy prices. Their site blocks automated requests, so I could not read a current price from the page itself, and a price I cannot verify is not a price. Look at the course page before you buy, and note that the marketplace runs discounts constantly, so the number you first see is rarely the number you pay.

### LinkedIn Learning, formerly Lynda.com

![Lynda-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Lynda.png)

_Lynda.com as it was. Everything on that site now lives at [LinkedIn Learning](https://www.linkedin.com/learning/)._

Lynda Weinman and Bruce Heavin's company was one of the originals in online video training, LinkedIn bought it, and the brand was folded into LinkedIn Learning. Typing lynda.com into a browser today just redirects you.

The platform now lists 25,900 courses and more than 1,300 curated learning paths, with a free first month. I have not put a subscription price in the table because LinkedIn will not show one without an account, and I am not going to print a figure I could not read.

What it is good at is breadth of software training, and the fact that a lot of employers already pay for it. Check whether you have access through work or a college before you pay for it yourself.

### Master.dev, formerly Frontend Masters

Frontend Masters became [Master.dev](https://master.dev/) on 15 June 2026. Same 300-plus courses, same instructors, broader catalogue that now runs past frontend into backend, cloud and AI. It costs $39 a month or $390 a year.

This is not a beginner platform and it is not trying to be. The courses are long, taught by working engineers, and assume you already write code. Buy a year of it when you have finished a free curriculum and want to go deep on one thing properly, not when you are still deciding whether you like this.

### Interaction Design Foundation

[IxDF](https://ixdf.org/) has been running since 2002 and now lists 41 courses and 159 master classes on UX, UI and product design. It also quietly moved house: interaction-design.org redirects to ixdf.org, so update any old bookmark.

Membership is Rs 650 a month for the Student plan or Rs 800 a month for Professional, both billed annually, with a 14-day money-back guarantee. At Indian pricing that is the cheapest structured UX theory on this list by a distance. The material is academic in tone, which is a strength if you want to understand why an interface works and a weakness if you want to be building by Friday.

### Flux Academy

![Flux Academy-home](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/09/Flux-Academy.png)

_Source: [flux academy](https://www.flux-academy.com/)_

Flux now sells membership rather than individual flagship courses. Foundation is $197 a quarter or $597 a year, Edge is $497 a quarter or $1,497 a year, and Accelerator is $1,667 a quarter or $4,997 a year, all with a 30-day money-back guarantee. The current catalogue covers the Webflow Masterclass, the Framer Masterclass, Figma For Web Designers, Web Design Masterclass, Brand Design Mastery, Core Design Skills, Freelancing for Web Designers, WriteSite and Web Strategy Master.

The honest framing: Flux teaches the commercial side of being a web designer, which is the part that decides whether you make a living. It is also priced in dollars, which makes it the most expensive thing on this list for anyone earning in rupees. Read the free YouTube material first and see whether the way he thinks matches how you want to work.

## Skills worth building, in order

Most skills lists for web designers are ten bullet points of nouns with no priority attached, which is useless to someone deciding what to open tonight. Here is the order I would defend.

1. **HTML and CSS, properly.** Not enough to follow a tutorial. Enough to build a layout from a blank file and know why it holds together. Everything else is easier afterwards.
2. **Layout and typography.** Spacing, hierarchy and type do more for how a site feels than colour or imagery ever will, and none of the platforms above teach this as well as looking hard at work you admire does.
3. **Figma.** Learn one interface design tool well rather than three badly, and make it the one your collaborators already use. The fundamentals transfer; tools keep dying.
4. **Responsive behaviour and accessibility.** These belong together because both come down to not assuming anything about the person on the other end.
5. **Enough JavaScript to be dangerous.** You do not need to be an engineer. You need to know what is cheap to ask for and what is expensive.
6. **The process around the work.** Briefing, scoping, review, handover. We wrote up how we run ours in our [web design process guide](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/web-design-process-guide/).

If you want the thinking behind the craft rather than the mechanics, the reading list in our [must-read books for web and graphic designers](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/13-top-must-read-books-for-web-and-graphic-designers/) covers ground no video course does.

## Questions people actually ask

### Do I have to learn to code to be a web designer?

You have to be able to read it. A designer who cannot read HTML and CSS produces work that developers quietly redraw, and never finds out why the built site does not match the file. You do not need to be able to build a backend.

### Is a paid subscription worth it if I am earning in rupees?

Not at the start. The three free resources at the top of this list are not a compromise, they are the recommendation. The subscriptions worth considering on Indian pricing are Codecademy and IxDF, both of which quote in rupees and land under Rs 1,000 a month billed annually. Dollar-priced platforms are a different order of expense, and I would only reach for one after free material has stopped being enough.

### Which certificate gets you hired?

My honest opinion is that the certificate is not what convinces anyone. Finished work is. A certificate says you completed a course; three real projects with an explanation of the decisions behind them says you can do the job. If you are choosing between them anyway, a university or company-branded certificate on Coursera carries a name a stranger recognises, which a platform-branded one does not.

### How long does this take?

freeCodeCamp puts each of its certifications at about 300 hours. At ten hours a week, one certification is most of a year. Anyone selling you a career in six weeks is selling you something else.

### Why are some prices here in rupees and some in dollars?

Because that is what each company showed me. Codecademy, Treehouse, Coursera and IxDF quote Indian pricing to a visitor from India. Master.dev, Flux Academy and Treehouse's Techdegree quote in dollars. I have kept each one in the currency it was displayed in rather than converting, because exchange rates move and a converted number would be wrong by the time you read this.

## Where to go from here

Pick one of freeCodeCamp or The Odin Project tonight, keep MDN in a tab, and do not open a second course until you have finished the first. The failure mode for self-taught designers is not choosing the wrong platform. It is choosing four of them.
