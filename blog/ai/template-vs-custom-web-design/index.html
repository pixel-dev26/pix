article

I sell custom web design for a living. Read the next sentence with that firmly in mind. Most of the businesses that walk into my studio in Salt Lake asking for a custom website would be better served by a template, and I say so before anyone signs anything.

That is not modesty. It is the only thing that makes a comparison like this worth reading. Every article on this question is written by somebody who sells one of the two answers, which is why they all arrive at the same one. So here is mine, stated up front and argued against my own invoice. A template is the right call when your website is a place to be found. It is the wrong call when your website is the thing that has to do the persuading.

The choice grew a third door, and the old question survived it

Until recently the decision was binary. Buy a theme, or commission a design. Then a third door opened. Wix, Squarespace and WordPress.com all open with a prompt box now, and the template gallery has been demoted to the fallback behind it.

I have made the full case on that elsewhere and will not repeat it here. The argument about distinctiveness, machine readability, ownership and what three years actually costs is in AI website builders versus custom development. What the vendors charge the moment you try to publish is in the upside and downside of building your website with AI.

What matters for this post is narrower. The prompt box did not add a third kind of website. It added a faster route through the first door. You still finish with a layout whose decisions were made before anybody knew what you sell. So the old question survives, restated: did anyone think about your specific business before the design existed, and does it cost you anything that they did not?

Template-based Web Design 

A template gives you preset options and combinations with the ability to change a few of them. Boxes to tick. The end result closely matches your choices, and it closely matches everyone else's too.

WordPress is the clearest case. Install it, pick a theme free or paid, then adjust: sections in and out, colours, fonts. Push that far enough and you get a site that looks like it was assembled by three people who never spoke to each other, which is the usual way a template project goes wrong.

There is one distinction almost nobody draws, and it decides more than the design does. Template now describes two different commercial arrangements. A WordPress theme is a purchase you keep: on ThemeForest today they run from roughly $19 to $99. A Wix or Squarespace template is not a purchase at all. It is included with a subscription and it stops being yours the day you stop paying. Wix's website plans run $17 to $159 a month on yearly billing, so even the cheapest one is a little over $200 a year, indefinitely. Cheap and rented are not the same word, and only one of them appears in the sales copy.

[IMG: A WordPress theme is bought once, roughly $19 to $99 on ThemeForest, and stays yours. A Wix or Squarespace template is rented with a $17 to $159 a month subscription, over $200 a year indefinitely, and stops being yours when you stop paying.]

Pros: 

– Inexpensive: the upfront cost is a fraction of a custom build. If your budget is genuinely tight, this is the honest option and I would rather you took it than bought half a custom site.

– Quick to set up: you are editing something that already exists rather than deciding what should exist, so a template site goes live in days.

– Offers a wide variety of choices: the marketplaces are enormous, and for most common business types someone has already built a competent version of your site.

– Shorter development cycle: the code is written and tested before you arrive. All you supply is content, which is also the step most people underestimate.

Cons: 

– Lack of originality: you are using a design that has been bought and deployed by other businesses, sometimes thousands of them, sometimes in your own city.

– Less flexible: a template is built to serve a category, not a company. It will accommodate most of what you want and refuse the specific thing that matters, and you usually discover which is which after you have committed.

– Constrained rather than unscalable: templates are commonly said not to scale. I think that is sloppy. Traffic is a hosting problem, not a theme problem, and a well-built theme handles growth fine. What does not scale is the design system: the day you need a section the template has no pattern for, you either bolt on something that looks foreign or you rebuild.

– Less control over the design process: you are choosing between finished answers instead of asking the questions, which is exactly the trade you are making and the reason it is cheaper.

– Security, but not the way my industry tells it: agencies like mine have sold the fear of the hacked free theme for years, and the data does not really support us. Patchstack's State of WordPress Security in 2026 counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities across the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, up 42% on the year before, and found 91% of them in plugins against 9% in themes. The risk is real and it is mostly not the template. It is what you bolt onto the template. Pirated themes from outside a marketplace remain a genuinely bad idea, and so does a site running fourteen plugins nobody has updated since installation.

Custom Web Design 

A custom design is built for one business and its actual situation. The process starts before any visual work: understanding what you sell, who you are selling against, who is buying, and what the site has to make them do. Only then is anything designed. Framework, navigation and back-end are shaped to fit that, rather than the other way round.

That sequence is the entire product. Anyone can produce a handsome page now, including a prompt box. What you are paying for is the thinking that happens before the page.

Pros: 

– Achieves your specific business goals: the design is built backwards from what the site is supposed to accomplish, so the features and functionality you need are in scope from the start rather than retrofitted.

– Attracts your target audience: the work begins with who you are talking to, and the site can be adapted as that audience or your positioning shifts.

– Increases brand awareness and recognition: a distinctive site is a memorable one, which is the whole mechanism behind brand awareness.

– Creates a good first impression and builds credibility: people decide whether to trust you before they read a word of your copy, and a site that clearly was not bought off a shelf does some of that work for you.

– Flexible and scalable: new sections, new products and new campaigns fit into a system that was designed to be extended, instead of being wedged into one that was not.

– Better control of what you run: you decide what code is on the site and what is not, which is a more honest version of the security claim than the one above.

Cons: 

– Costly: it is a professional service and it is priced like one. I refuse to print an unsourced price range here, for the reasons I set out in what web design actually costs in Kolkata, where I take apart the numbers other agencies publish.

– Takes time to build: three months is my honest minimum, and that assumes communication does not stall. Goals, deliverables, wireframes, design, development, testing, optimisation, with several people working in parallel.

– Requires skilled designers: which means the outcome depends heavily on who you hire, and hiring badly is more expensive than buying a template. The questions worth asking before you hire an agency exist because of that.

What determines your choice of web design?

Deciding how to get your website built is not only a question of budget or of the right creative idea. It is a blend of those and a few other things.

[IMG: Seven criteria and which answer each points at: a simple site, a general market and a launch this month point at a template; interactive content, a niche audience, unusual workflows and a presence recognisably yours point at a custom build.]

1. Budget

You will be told your website budget should be about 1 to 2% of annual revenue before a custom build makes sense. Ignore it. I could not find a source for that figure anywhere, and a benchmark with no study behind it is worse than no benchmark, because clients use it as a negotiating anchor against a real quote. What is actually true is duller: work out what the site has to earn or save, and if that number is small, buy a template without guilt.

2. Complexity

How complex does the site need to be? Lots of interactive content or serious e-commerce points at custom. A simple site with no frills points at a template.

3. Target Audience

Understand who you are designing for before you design anything. A custom build gives far more control over design, development and functionality. If you serve a specific niche that needs a solution templates do not carry, that control is the point. If you serve a general market that expects a conventional site, a template will do the job and nobody will notice.

4. Flexibility and Extensibility

Templates aim at a generalised solution, which is exactly why they cannot be very flexible. If your plans include adding functions or processes later, a custom site can be designed with that in mind from the start. A template can be extended, but every extension is a negotiation with somebody else's decisions.

5. Outcome

Identity contributes heavily to a brand and its image. If the priority is establishing a presence that is recognisably yours, a template does not offer the level of personalisation that needs. Nothing stops you making a template site as distinctive as you can, but it was not built to be. A custom site can reflect the culture and values that separate you from everyone else selling the same thing.

6. Time

If you need something live quickly, a template wins and it is not close. Custom work takes months for the reasons listed above, and rushing it mostly means skipping the thinking you were paying for.

7. Functionality

Simple transactions are within a template's reach. Serious e-commerce, unusual workflows or anything that has to talk to your other systems is not, and the point where a template stops being cheaper is the point where you start paying somebody to fight it.

So which one, honestly

Buy a template if the site's job is to exist, to be found, and to answer a few obvious questions. Buy a template if the budget is tight, because a good template beats a compromised custom build every time. Buy a template if you need to be live this month.

Commission a custom design when the website is a channel rather than an address, when being indistinguishable from your competitors costs you money, or when the thing you need the site to do is specific enough that nobody has built it in advance.

Most businesses need the first thing and are sold the second. That is the bias in this whole category, mine included, and you should weigh what I have written against the fact that I earn more when you pick custom. What I will defend either way is the sequence. Design was never the pixels. It is the questions asked before the pixels, and you can pay for those or skip them, on a template or otherwise.

At Pixel Street, we are a web design company in Kolkata that does the asking first. If after reading this you think a template is your answer, take it, and I will still tell you the same thing: decide what the site has to do before you decide what it looks like.
