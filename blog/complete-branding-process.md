# A Comprehensive Guide to Branding Process

Every prospective client asks what our process is, and almost nobody asks the question they actually mean, which is: at what point do I lose the ability to change my mind cheaply? That is the only thing a branding process is really for. It is a schedule of decisions, arranged so the expensive ones happen after the cheap ones, and so nobody is redesigning a logo in week nine because the positioning was never settled in week two.

I run Pixel Street, a design and branding studio in Salt Lake, Kolkata. We work with brands including Coca-Cola, ITC and Marico, and before Pixel Street I lost two companies of my own. So this is written from the studio side of the table by someone who has also been the client paying for a rework. I have tried to describe what happens at each stage and what tends to go wrong there, rather than what each stage is called.

## The short version

Branding runs in two halves. The first is strategic and produces a document: who you are for, what you stand against, what you will not do. The second is expressive and produces assets: identity, messaging, guidelines, the things people actually see. Doing the second before the first is the single most common and most expensive mistake in this business, and it is expensive because visual work is where the money goes.

This page is the engagement in order. The strategy document itself, and how to write one that rules things out, is [the guide to building a brand strategy](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/guide-to-build-a-brand-strategy/). Whether you should be trying to break your category at all is [the case for and against a disruptive brand](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/building-a-disruptive-brand/). If what you are really doing is changing an existing brand rather than starting one, go to [the rebranding roadmap](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/the-ultimate-rebranding-roadmap/) instead, because the sequence is different and the risks are larger.

| Stage | What gets decided | How it usually fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Strategy | Audience, position, what you refuse | Written so agreeably it rules nothing out |
| 2. Identity | Logo, colour, type, imagery | Started before stage one is signed |
| 3. Messaging | Name lines, proof, tone | Tagline written by committee |
| 4. Assets | Site, packaging, collateral | Scope discovered late, one item at a time |
| 5. Guidelines | Rules for reuse | Delivered as a PDF nobody opens |
| 6. Communication | Channels and cadence | Channel chosen first, position retrofitted |
| 7. Review | What changed in perception | Measured against nothing recorded at the start |
| 8. Advocacy | How customers repeat you | Treated as a campaign, not an outcome |

## Understanding Branding

A brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room. That definition is old and slightly worn, and it survives because it puts the brand outside the company, where it belongs. You do not own it. You own the inputs.

Which is why I am wary of the benefit lists this kind of page usually carries: builds trust, commands premium pricing, increases recall, drives loyalty. Each of those is a claim about what customers will do, made in a document written by the company. They are outcomes you might earn, not features you have bought. Branding work makes them possible; it does not deliver them, and no studio that tells you otherwise can show you the arithmetic.

What the work does reliably do is narrower and worth paying for. It makes you legible. A person who encounters you three times in three places should be able to tell it was the same company, understand roughly what you sell, and form some opinion about whether it is for them. Most small companies fail that test, not because their design is poor but because every touchpoint was made by a different person on a different day with no rule to follow.

### The four things being built

1. **Brand identity:** the name, mark, colour, type and imagery. The part everyone means when they say branding, and a smaller share of the job than they expect.
2. **Brand personality:** the human traits attached to the company, which decide how the identity is used rather than what it looks like.
3. **Brand messaging:** the sentences. What you say first, what you say when challenged, what you never claim.
4. **Brand experience:** everything the customer actually touches, which is where most brands quietly contradict their own guidelines.

## **Branding Process**

Eight stages, in the order they have to happen. The order matters more than the stage names; every one of them depends on a decision made in the one before it, which is why skipping ahead feels fast and costs more.

![The eight stages run in order from strategy to advocacy, and the cost of changing your mind widens as you go: a decision changed in stage one costs a meeting, and the same decision changed in stage four costs a reprint, a rebuild and an apology.](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/diagrams/complete-branding-process-cost-of-change.svg)

### Step 1: Define Your Brand Strategy

Purpose, audience, position, and the things you are ruling out. This stage produces a document, not a design, and its quality is measured by what it forbids. A strategy that a competitor could adopt unchanged has decided nothing.

The stage goes wrong when it is run as a workshop that everyone enjoys. Agreeable sessions produce agreeable documents. The useful version of this meeting has an argument in it, usually about a customer segment somebody in the room does not want to give up. That argument is the deliverable. [The brand strategy guide](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/guide-to-build-a-brand-strategy/) sets out the questions worth having it over.

### Step 2: Craft Your Brand Identity

![Elements of Brand Identity.](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2023/07/Elements-of-Brand-Identity-1.png)

Now the strategy becomes something you can see. Identity is the mark, the shapes around it, the palette, the typefaces and the imagery, and they are chosen together because they only work together.

#### **Brand logo:**

The mark carries recognition and nothing else. It does not explain what you sell and it should not try. The tests I actually apply are the boring ones: does it survive black and white, does it survive sixteen pixels, does it survive being cropped to a circle by a social platform that did not ask you. This is one stage inside the wider engagement and it has its own sequence, which is in [the logo design process](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/logo-design-process/).

#### **Shape:**

Shape work extends past the mark into backgrounds, layouts, packaging and cards. It is the cheapest element to be consistent about and the one most often left undecided, which is why so many small brands have a considered logo sitting on top of arbitrary boxes.

#### **Colour scheme and palette:**

Pick a palette, write it down as values, and use it everywhere. That is the whole requirement, and it is harder than it sounds because the palette has to survive a printer, a phone at low brightness and somebody in accounts using the wrong blue in a spreadsheet.

What I will not do here is tell you what colours mean. Colour psychology in marketing is the least reliable material in the whole field: the recognisable figures, including the claim that colour lifts brand recognition by up to eighty per cent, trace back through a chain of citations to a colour consultancy's own web page and stop there, and the researcher usually named alongside the number published on how people read pie charts. Choose colours that distinguish you from the specific competitors a customer sees you next to. That is a real constraint. The rest is decoration dressed as science.

#### **Brand voice:**

Voice is a constraint, not a style. Write it as pairs, because a single adjective gives a copywriter nothing to push against: warm but not chatty, expert but not lecturing, confident but not loud. Then apply it to something hard, like a refund refusal or a price rise. Voice guidelines that only cover the good news are untested.

![brand personality](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/media/2022/08/bramnd20personality-e1659591065983.webp)

#### **Positioning and style:**

Positioning is decided in stage one; what happens here is translating it into a style somebody else can follow. Language choices, tone, how products are described, what a photograph is allowed to look like. If two people write a product description independently and the results do not sound related, the style guide is not finished.

#### **Typography and imagery:**

Choose type for reading first and character second. A face that is distinctive at poster size and unreadable in a nine-point form has failed, because the form is where your customers spend their time. Check it at the smallest size you will ever set it, on the worst screen you can find, before you fall in love with the specimen. There is more on this in [the principles of typography](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/top-10-principles-of-typography-in-design/).

Imagery is the element clients underestimate. Gradients, patterns, packaging and photography carry more of the recognition load than the logo does, because there is more of them on any given page.

#### **Interactions:**

Every company has a service voice whether it has written one or not. Support email, phone manner, the tone of a complaint reply, the message on a delayed order. These are usually written by whoever is on shift, and they are where the brand is contradicted most often, because nobody sends the guidelines to the person answering at nine at night.

#### **UX and UI:**

The site is where the identity meets the actual constraint of use. Interface work has its own rules, and they beat brand preferences every time they conflict: a colour that fails contrast is not a brand asset, it is an inaccessible one. Where a guideline and usability disagree, change the guideline.

#### **Sound:**

Sound matters if you advertise in video or audio, and not much otherwise. Decide it when you need it. Most small brands are told to build a sonic identity years before they have anywhere to play it.

### Step 3: Create Brand Messaging

Messaging is what you say, in what order, and what you refuse to claim. The first line a stranger reads about you is worth more work than the logo and gets a fraction of it.

**Tagline or slogan**

A tagline is optional. I say that at the start of this conversation because clients often arrive believing it is compulsory, and a bad one is worse than none: it takes up the most valuable line on the page to say something no competitor would dispute.

Three famous taglines get listed as evidence for this, and the list is a better argument than the one it is usually making. Nike launched _Just Do It_ in 1988, and when Nike ran a campaign around it in September 2025 the company's own announcement called that a reintroduction, not a replacement. Coca-Cola's _Taste the Feeling_ arrived in 2016 and was retired in September 2021, when Real Magic was announced as the first new global platform for the trademark since 2016. Subway's _Eat Fresh_ was absorbed into a menu campaign called the Eat Fresh Refresh in July 2021 and has been reworked regionally since.

So of the three lines this page once called timeless, one has survived thirty-seven years and two were changed within a decade — and I only found that out by checking, which nobody does. That is the useful test. A line that is genuinely working gets kept by its owner for decades against considerable internal pressure to do something new. Most lines are not working, which is why most of them get replaced quietly.

### Step 4: Build Brand Assets

Business cards, packaging, decks, signage, the website. This is the stage where budgets are actually spent, and where scope arrives late and one item at a time.

The way to control it is to list every asset before design starts, including the ones nobody thinks of: the invoice, the email signature, the sticker on the carton, the banner for a trade stand in a hall with bad lighting. An asset discovered after the guidelines are signed gets designed by whoever is free, and that is how a brand starts drifting in month two.

### Step 5: Establish Brand Guidelines

Guidelines exist so that people who were not in any of these meetings can make something correct without asking. Judge them by that test alone.

Most brand guidelines fail it, because they are written as a document about the brand rather than as instructions for a stranger. A junior marketer needs the colour values in a form they can paste, the logo in the file types they will be asked for, the two type sizes that cover most cases, and a page of things not to do with worked examples. A long manifesto about brand essence is a portfolio piece for the agency that made it.

### Step 6: Implement Brand Communication

Now the identity meets the market. Channels, cadence, who publishes, who approves. The rule from stage one applies here and gets ignored here: the channel is chosen because the position points at it, not the other way round.

The practical failure at this stage is the approval chain. If four people can veto a post and nobody can commission one, output collapses to whatever is uncontroversial, which is exactly what a distinctive position was supposed to avoid. Name the person who can say yes before the first post goes out.

### Step 7: Monitor and Evolve Your Brand

You cannot measure a change in perception without a record of what perception was before. Take the measurement in stage one, when it is boring to do and nobody wants to: what customers currently say you are, what they think you cost, which competitor they name next to you.

Then be patient with the clock. Positioning shows up in what people say about you unprompted, and that lags by quarters, not weeks. Anyone offering a thirty-day proof point is measuring a campaign.

Distinguish maintenance from change. Adjusting how a position is expressed is normal and should happen continuously. Changing the position is a rebrand, with the costs and risks set out in [the rebranding roadmap](https://pixelstreet.in/blog/the-ultimate-rebranding-roadmap/).

### Step 8: Foster Brand Advocacy

Advocacy is an outcome, not a stage you can run. What you can do is make yourself easy to repeat. If a customer cannot describe you in one sentence to a friend, they will not, however satisfied they are.

That sentence is the real test of everything above. Write down what you want it to be at the start of the project, then ask three actual customers at the end and compare. The gap between the two is the honest scorecard for the whole engagement.

## Common questions about the branding process

### Can I skip straight to the logo?

You can, and people do. What you are buying is a mark chosen against no criteria, which means the review meeting becomes a taste contest and the result gets revisited within a year. If budget is the constraint, do a small strategy stage rather than none.

### Do I need all eight stages?

The order is compulsory; the depth is not. A three-person company needs stages one, two, three and five in a light form, and can defer four and six until there is something to spend them on. What does not work is running them out of sequence.

### What does the client actually have to supply?

Access, mostly. Time with whoever makes decisions, honest answers about competitors and pricing, and a named decision-maker who can approve without a committee. Projects stall on availability far more often than on creative disagreement.

### How do I know the guidelines worked?

Hand them to somebody who was not involved and ask them to produce something simple, like a social post or a one-page flyer. Whatever they get wrong is a defect in the guidelines, not in them.

## Where I would start

If you take one thing from this page, take the order. Strategy, then identity, then messaging, then everything else. The reason it is worth being stubborn about is financial rather than aesthetic: decisions made in stage one cost a meeting to change, and the same decisions made in stage four cost a reprint, a rebuild and an apology to a client who already approved something.
